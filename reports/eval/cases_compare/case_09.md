When, in a case of delegation, the contracting parties 
agree to substitute a new obligation for the original 
obligation, such a delegation constitutes a novation by 
the change of the debtor. It results in the liberation of 
the original debtor from the obligation to his creditor, 
provided that the new obligation assumed by the new

absence of an agreement providing for novation, the 
original obligation continues concurrently with the new

## REFERENCE
Article 360 of the Egyptian Civil Code addresses the legal effects of a delegation (where a third party assumes the original debtor's obligation), distinguishing the case where the parties expressly agree to substitute the new obligation for the old (true novation) from the default case where both obligations remain in force.

Article 360 of the Egyptian Civil Code provides:

* **Novation requires express agreement:** A delegation amounts to a novation by change of debtor only when the contracting parties agree to substitute a new obligation for the original obligation; without such agreement, no novation occurs.
* **Effect of valid novation:** When novation is agreed, the original debtor is liberated from the obligation to the creditor — but only if (a) the new obligation assumed by the new debtor is valid, and (b) the new debtor is not insolvent at the time of the delegation.
* **No presumption of novation:** Novation is never presumed in a case of delegation; in the absence of a clear agreement to novate, the default applies.
* **Default: both obligations coexist:** Without novation, the original obligation continues concurrently with the new obligation, giving the creditor two debtors instead of one — typically a stronger position.

Example: A buyer arranges for a friend to take over his car-loan obligation to a bank. If the bank, the friend, and the buyer all agree expressly to release the buyer and accept only the friend, that is a novation under Article 360 — the buyer is liberated. If they don't expressly agree to release the buyer, the buyer remains liable alongside the friend, and the bank can pursue either.

## BASELINE (Stage 0) PREDICTION
Article 360 of the Egyptian Civil Code provides that a person who has lost a personal property must give notice to the owner within a reasonable time after the loss, and that failure to do so bars the right to claim compensation.

Article 360 of the Egyptian Civil Code provides:

* **Purpose**: To ensure that the owner is aware of the loss and can take steps to prevent further damage.
* **Requirement**: The person who lost the property must notify the owner within a reasonable time after the loss.
* **Reasonable time**: The time frame is not specified, but it should be short enough for the owner to respond without undue delay.
* **Failure to notify**: If the person fails to notify the owner within the reasonable time, they lose their right to claim compensation from the owner.
* **Example**: A car owner discovers that her vehicle has been stolen. She should immediately notify the police and the car dealer (the owner) to avoid losing the right to claim compensation under Article 360.

## STAGE-1 PREDICTION
Article 360 of the Egyptian Civil Code addresses the effect of a sale that is not completed when the contract is voidable or subject to an exemption clause. It provides that the buyer's obligation to pay is extinguished at the time of the contract's nullification or exemption, and that the seller's right to retain the price is likewise extinguished.

Article 360 of the Egyptian Civil Code provides:

* **Nullification of the Contract:** When the contract is voidable (either because of lack of capacity, lack of consent, or other grounds), the buyer's obligation to pay is extinguished at the moment the contract becomes void.
* **Exemption Clause:** If the contract is exempt from the general rule (e.g., due to a special exemption clause in the agreement), the same rule applies — the buyer's obligation is extinguished at the moment of exemption.
* **Effect on Seller's Right:** Conversely, the seller's right to retain the price is also extinguished at the moment the buyer's obligation is extinguished; the seller cannot claim the money back once it has been paid out.
* **Practical Implication:** A buyer who has already paid under a voidable contract loses the money; the seller loses the right to keep it. This preserves the integrity of the market and prevents unjust enrichment for either party.

Example: A buyer buys a car from a seller with a valid exemption clause. The buyer pays the full price. Under Article 360, the buyer's obligation to pay is extinguished at the moment the exemption clause comes into effect. The seller's right to retain the price is also extinguished. The buyer can no longer reclaim the payment; the seller cannot demand it back.
