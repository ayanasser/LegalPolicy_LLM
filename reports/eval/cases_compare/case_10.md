## REFERENCE
Article 535 of the Egyptian Civil Code regulates what a liquidator of a partnership can and cannot do. Their authority is bounded by the wind-up purpose, but, within those limits, they have broad freedom to sell partnership assets — subject to restrictions in their appointment instrument.

Article 535 of the Egyptian Civil Code provides:

* **Liquidator's mandate is closure-oriented** — The role exists to wind down the partnership, not to grow it or restart its business.
* **No new business undertaking** — As a default rule, the liquidator must not undertake new transactions on behalf of the partnership.
* **Permitted necessity exception** — A new transaction is permissible if it is necessary to terminate existing business — for example, a small purchase needed to complete a contract already in progress.
* **Sale authority over assets** — The liquidator may sell both movables and immovables of the partnership.
* **Two sale modalities** — They may use either public auction or private treaty, with the choice generally driven by which yields the better price under the circumstances.
* **Restriction by appointment instrument** — The contract or court order appointing the liquidator can limit these sale powers (e.g., requiring auction only, requiring partner consent above a threshold).
* **Practical implication** — Partners drafting an appointment instrument can tailor the liquidator's powers carefully; absent such tailoring, the broad default applies.

Example: After dissolution of a manufacturing partnership, the liquidator wants to sell the factory. Under Article 535, they may do so by private treaty — useful if a strategic buyer offers a premium — unless the appointment instrument restricts them to public auction. They cannot, however, accept a fresh long-term supply contract to keep the factory running.

## BASELINE (Stage 0) PREDICTION
Article 535 of the Egyptian Civil Code provides that a person who has been injured by another's act or omission may recover damages from the other party only if he can prove that the act or omission was the cause of his injury.

Article 535 of the Egyptian Civil Code provides:

* **Purpose**: To establish the principle that a person must prove causation before recovering damages for an injury caused by another's act or omission.
* **Rule**: A person who has been injured by another's act or omission may recover damages only if he can prove that the act or omission was the cause of his injury.
* **Exception**: The rule does not apply where the injury is caused by the act or omission itself (e.g., falling off a cliff).
* **Application**: This article applies to all types of injuries, including physical, mental, and emotional harm.
* **Example**: A pedestrian is hit by a car while crossing the street. The pedestrian cannot recover damages under Article 535 because the car's act was the cause of the injury; it was the vehicle that struck the pedestrian, not the pedestrian's act of crossing the street.
* **Practical implications**: The article helps prevent unjust enrichment where one party causes another's injury without compensation, and encourages parties to take care when acting in the public interest.

## STAGE-1 PREDICTION
Article 535 of the Egyptian Civil Code addresses the legal consequences of a contract that is not fully performed — whether it remains valid and enforceable or becomes void. The rule is designed to protect parties from being penalized for contractual mistakes.

Article 535 of the Egyptian Civil Code provides:

* **General rule** — A contract that is not fully performed is considered valid and enforceable.
* **Reason for the rule** — Contracts are flexible; they can be modified over time.
* **Exception for void contracts** — If the contract is void (e.g., because of fraud, illegality), the non-performance does not affect its validity.
* **Effect on third parties** — The non-performing party is not held responsible for the contract's failure.
* **Why the rule works** — Flexibility in contract terms allows for adjustments without invalidating the entire agreement.

Example: A buyer and seller agree to sell a car for EGP 100,000. The buyer pays EGP 80,000 but cannot pay the remaining EGP 20,000. Under Article 535, the contract remains valid and enforceable. The buyer is not held responsible for the unpaid balance; the seller must find another buyer or settle the debt themselves.
