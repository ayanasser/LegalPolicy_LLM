## REFERENCE
Article 280 of the Egyptian Civil Code governs how a debtor can satisfy a debt held in solidarity by multiple creditors, and what happens to a creditor's share when they die. It balances the debtor's practical convenience against the right of any creditor to insist that payment be made to them.

Article 280 of the Egyptian Civil Code provides that, when there is solidarity between creditors, the debtor may pay the debt to any one of them unless one of them objects; and that solidarity does not prevent the debt from being divided between the heirs of one of the joint and several creditors, unless the debt itself is indivisible.

To walk through the article:

1. Default flexibility for the debtor. The general rule benefits the debtor: when several creditors share solidarity, paying any one of them in full is enough to discharge the obligation. The chosen creditor's receipt is binding on the others.

2. Veto by objection. The article explicitly preserves the right of any creditor to object to the debtor's chosen payee. Once an objection is raised, the debtor can no longer choose freely; they must direct payment differently to avoid making an invalid payment.

3. Inheritance and division. The death of a joint and several creditor breaks up that creditor's share. The share passes to the heirs as a divisible debt by default. This contrasts with the original solidarity, which is not inherited.

4. Indivisibility carries forward. If the debt itself is indivisible (for example, the delivery of a unique object), division among heirs does not work. The heirs must continue to act collectively for that share.

Example: Three joint and several creditors are owed 60,000 EGP. The debtor pays any one of them in full unless objected to. If a creditor dies leaving two heirs, that 20,000 EGP share splits 10,000 between them.
